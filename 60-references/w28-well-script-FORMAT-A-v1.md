30 Things Every American Farm Well Had to Do

Five in the morning, and the first sound on the place is iron. The pump handle comes down, the leathers catch,
and water starts climbing a pipe that has been climbing it since before anyone now living was born. On most
American farms before rural water arrived, that handle was the entire water supply: the drinking, the cooking,
the washing, the milk, the herd, the garden, the fire barrel and the butter for next week. A farm well was not a
fixture. It was a job with thirty parts, and every one of them ran on somebody's arms.

Here are the thirty things a farm well had to do, and what it actually took to do them.

DRINKING, COOKING AND WASHING

It had to supply every drop the household drank. There was no tap and no jug from the store. A farm family of
six moved somewhere between thirty and forty gallons a day, and the only machine anywhere in that chain was a
two-gallon bucket and whoever stood closest to it.

It had to put hot water in the kitchen without a boiler. The reservoir set into the side of the kitchen range
held ten or twelve gallons, was filled by hand, and drew its heat off the same fire that cooked the dinner. It
was the closest thing on the farm to a tap.

It had to carry washing day. A copper boiler took ten to fifteen gallons up to the boil, softened with a
spoonful of borax, and a single wash of a farm family's clothes could take four or five changes of water before
the rinse water was clean enough to use.

It had to fill the Saturday tub. Bath night ran oldest to youngest on the same water, with a final two-gallon
pour from a dipper as the rinse. The well did not set how clean anyone got. It set how many people got through.

It had to take the dirty water back out again. Every dishpan, scrub bucket and wash boiler went to the garden or
the stock trough, because clean water was too much work to throw on the ground. A farm reused its water until
there was nothing left to reuse it for.

THE MILK MONEY

It had to cool the milk before it turned. The cream check was the farm's cash income, and warm milk sours in a
few hours in summer. Cans went into a trough of cold spring water in the well house, and that trough was the
difference between shipping cream to town and feeding it to the hogs.

It had to rinse the separator. The cream separator had eighteen discs that came apart after every milking, twice
a day, and any film of milk left on one of them would sour the whole batch of cream by morning. It was washed in
water hauled by hand, in the dark, before anyone had eaten.

It had to chill the butter. Churning took the cream past the point where it broke into butter and buttermilk,
and cold spring water both set the grain and washed the buttermilk out so the butter would keep past a week.
Warm water made butter that went rancid in days.

It had to help the cans get to town. On the wagon run, wet burlap over the cans and wind moving across it pulled
the temperature down several degrees an hour. A farmer who timed the run properly sold cream that was still
sweet at the station, and one who did not sold cream that was already turning.

It had to keep the eggs. Before refrigeration, eggs went down into a barrel of water glass, sodium silicate
thinned with water, which sealed the shell and held them good for six to eight months. The well house gave them
the cool, dark, steady place they needed to sit in.

WATER FOR THE HERD

It had to water the milking herd. One cow in milk drinks ten to fifteen gallons a day. Thirty head meant three
hundred to four hundred and fifty gallons every twenty-four hours, pumped by hand into a trough in every kind of
weather, including the weather nobody wanted to be outside in.

It had to water the teams that did the work. A horse in harness drinks ten to twelve gallons a day and will not
work long without it. The field trough was refilled by hand, or the team was walked back to the well at noon and
watered before the afternoon's work started.

It had to serve the hogs. A hog drinks three to five gallons a day and cannot sweat, which is why the wallow
mattered as much as the trough and why both of them came out of the same pipe. On a hot day the hogs were the
heaviest draw on the well after the cattle.

It had to keep the poultry alive. Chickens drink more in a hot week than most people expect, and a fount left
dry for a single July day can cost a flock. Topping it up twice a day was a child's job on most farms, and it
was the first chore handed out.

It had to stop on its own when the tank was full. The float valve in the stock tank shut the flow the moment the
water reached its mark, which meant the well could fill a tank all day while everyone was out in the field,
without a single bucket being carried by anybody.

WATER FOR THE GROUND

It had to water the kitchen garden through a dry week. A garden thirty feet by fifty needs about an inch of
water a week, which is roughly nine hundred gallons. Before there was a hose, every one of those gallons came up
the pipe and crossed the yard in cans.

It had to keep young trees alive. Orchard trees and the windbreak saplings planted against the prairie wind need
steady water through their first three or four summers. One dry August would take the whole planting, and the
planting was supposed to outlast the man who made it.

It had to set the plants. Every flat of tomatoes, cabbage and peppers went into the ground with a dipper of water
poured into the hole first, a technique old enough to have its own name. Puddling in settled the roots into wet
soil instead of dry, and it was the difference between a plant that took and one that sulked.

It had to swell the wooden wheels. Iron tires were fitted to a wheel while hot and shrunk on as they cooled. If
the wood dried out, the tire came loose and the wheel came apart. Wheels and barrel staves went into the pond or
the trough to swell back tight, which is why a dry summer was hard on every wagon on the place.

It had to wash the mud off. Tools, milk cans, separators and hands all came clean off the same supply. In spring
a farm could spend more water on the equipment than it spent on the people, and nobody counted it as waste,
because a dirty separator or a rusty tool cost more than a bucket of water.

FIRE, HEAT AND COLD

It had to fight fire. A barn fire could take every animal and the entire year's feed in under an hour. A barrel
of water and a box of wet sand stood at every barn door, and the first water onto a fire came from whatever was
closest, which on most farms meant the well.

It had to wet down a dry roof. In a long hot spell, when the shingles and the haymow had the last of their
moisture baked out of them, somebody drew water and soaked the roof and the edges of the mow before a spark
found them. It was cheap insurance against the one fire that ruined a farm.

It had to be the only cold place on the farm. With no icebox and no electricity, the well house and the bottom
of the cistern were where milk, butter, meat and medicine went. How long food kept was a direct function of how
close the well house stood to the kitchen door.

It had to thaw itself in winter. Frost drives three to four feet down in the northern states. Straw bales and
manure banked against the pump house kept the pipe above freezing, and a pump that froze overnight split its
cylinder, which meant pulling the whole thing and starting again.

It had to scald. Butchering, rendering lard, scalding a hog and washing out the crocks all needed water close to
boiling, and it arrived at the tub two gallons at a time, carried by somebody who had already carried water all
day.

KEEPING THE WELL ALIVE

It had to hold a pump's leathers. The leather packing on the plunger of a hand pump wore out and had to be
swapped for fresh leather sealed with neatsfoot oil. A pump worked dry would ruin a set of leathers inside a
week, which is why a pump was never run dry if anyone was standing there to stop it.

It had to lift water further than a pump can pull. A suction pump can only lift water about twenty-five feet,
because that is all the atmosphere will give it. That single limit is why farm wells had to stay shallow, and
why a well that dropped a little lower every summer eventually needed a completely different machine.

It had to keep its prime. A pump that sits and drains loses the water seal inside its cylinder, and a pump full
of air lifts nothing at all. Priming meant pouring water down the top to start it, which sounds absurd and was
the reason nobody ever let a pump stand dry overnight.

It had to be sealed against the ground it stands in. The casing has to stand above the grass so surface water
sheets away, the cap has to be tight with a screened vent so the well can breathe, and the ground around it has
to slope away rather than in. A well that takes surface water takes the barnyard down with it.

It had to have a second source behind it. The cistern, roof water run through gutters into a plastered tank,
was what carried a farm through the weeks the well went low, went bad or went dry. A farm with one source of
water was always one dry summer away from hauling it in.

For most of the country that handle stopped being the only answer somewhere between the 1930s and the 1960s, as
the rural electric cooperatives ran their lines and pipe came up the road behind them. By 1970 a pump handle on
a farm was mostly decoration, and the well house was a shed you threw tools into. What went with it was the
arithmetic. A family knew exactly what a day's water cost, because every gallon of it had already gone past
their own shoulders. The electric pump never asks for that. It comes up the pipe, silent and unmeasured, and
almost nobody alive today can tell you precisely what a day's water weighs.

On plenty of farms the old casing is still standing a foot above the grass, capped and screened and dry, in the
same yard as the line the co-op buried. The handle is usually long gone. The foot of it, more often than not, is
still bolted to the same oak.
