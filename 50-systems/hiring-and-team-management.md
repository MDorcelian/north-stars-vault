# Hiring and Team Management SOP

Source: WannerCashCow group coaching call, 2026-07-31, hosted by Emanuele Olivetti. Full transcript on file. This is the operating doctrine North Stars should apply to all hiring and team management across Explainer Chris, Ace Knows a Guy, and Jiggy.

## Core principle: maximize raw profit, not just ROI
A great ROI (e.g. 9x on $800 -> $5,000/mo) can still mean under-scaling. Doubling investment to $1,600 that returns $8,000/mo (lower ROI, ~4x) nets $6,400/mo profit vs $4,200/mo at the smaller scale. When a channel can absorb more investment, chase the bigger absolute profit number even if the ROI ratio drops. Directly relevant to the CM hire and any future team scaling decisions.

## Why hire (pick the right frame per role)
- **Access to skill you lack**: voice actors, advanced editors, scriptwriters. You're buying quality, not just time.
- **Save your time**: VAs, repetitive tasks (uploads, tags, descriptions, community management). You could do it, but shouldn't.
- **Scale a role you already have**: hire a second/third of the same role; have the senior person train the junior.
- **Channel manager**: the advanced role. Often ends up outperforming the founder because they have dedicated time/focus and sometimes better risk tolerance or research skill. Not just time-saving, potentially a real upgrade.

## Where to hire, by need
- **OnlineJobs.ph**: Filipino workers, best for long-term continuous roles (editors, scriptwriters, VAs), fewer ultra-advanced specialists. Pay a subscription, but you can take relationships off-platform freely.
- **Upwork**: faster start, bigger global pool, higher-skill ceiling, but Upwork/Fiverr penalize (can terminate your account) if caught taking people off-platform.
- **Fiverr**: cheap, quick, one-off gigs, not for continuous relationships.
- **Discord / niche communities**: best when you need topic KNOWLEDGE, not just a skill, e.g. a GTA-lore-fluent scriptwriter/consultant for Ace Knows a Guy. Emanuele's direct advice when asked live about a GTA channel manager search: for GTA specifically, look for a VA you can train on channel management, not a ready-made "channel manager"; separately, source GTA knowledge/ideation help from GTA Discords or Reddit, then train them on YouTube technical skills. Channel manager itself is a technical/YouTube-literacy role first.

## Screening candidates
- Don't over-optimize on raw skill between two passable candidates; weigh effort signals: did they check your channel/job post, is the message personalized vs AI-templated, past reviews, responsiveness, whether they proactively bring ideas.
- Red flag: a candidate who runs their own YouTube channel / is an aspiring YouTuber themselves. Emanuele's direct answer to this exact question live on the call: treat it as a real red flag, they're not a reliable long-term freelancer, they're a competitor-in-training. **Directly relevant**: re-examine any current or past CM/editor candidate who has their own channel.
- Use tests/samples over interviews (interviews let people talk around gaps). Don't pay for tests except for high-level specialist roles (e.g. 3D animation on Upwork). Keep tests short: e.g. one minute of editing with script/voice already provided.
- Be wary of hiring "ambitious" people into roles with no room for growth (a VA role, for instance). Ambitious people in low-ceiling roles tend to job-hop, push for early raises, or use you as a stepping stone. Ambition is the RIGHT fit for a channel-manager-with-revenue-share role, wrong fit for a repetitive VA role.

## Compensation structures
- Revenue-share/percentage works well for channel managers (full control over performance = fair to tie their pay to outcomes).
- Risk: giving revenue share to a role WITHOUT full control (e.g. a scriptwriter when editing is actually what's carrying the channel) creates unfair upside/downside mismatches in both directions.

## Once hired: efficiency
- Do 1-2 videos yourself first (or hire briefly on a gig basis) to learn realistic timing before committing to full-time hires, so you can sanity-check whether a new hire is actually slow or the task is just hard.
- First 1-2 weeks of any full-time hire: high communication, End-of-Day (EOD) reports. Have them report not just output but process (e.g. "voice took longer because the AI take was low quality and needed heavy editing"). This builds your own institutional knowledge of the workflow, not just accountability.
- EOD reports: lightweight, no rigid structure needed (Discord/WhatsApp message, or a shared sheet/Notion calendar they fill in). Keep even with people you trust, it maintains accountability passively.
- Time tracking (screenshot software): generally not recommended, it's high-maintenance for you and lowers morale/trust. Use only if worried about being burned early, frame it as "helping me learn the workflow," not surveillance, and remove it once trust is established.
- Give team members competitor references and comparison videos to study rather than trying to give feedback yourself if you're not confident giving it. Teach them to do their own niche research on a routine cadence (e.g. weekly) rather than staying in a bubble.
- SOPs: record as a Loom (screen recording) while you do the task yourself narrating your thought process, not a written doc. Reusable for every future hire in that role, and survives someone quitting/being fired.
- Keep workflow tooling simple at small team size (1-2 people): direct messaging beats a full Trello/PM system. Only add structured systems (multiple tools, board tracking) once the team grows past ~5 people or you have multiple channel managers.

## Human side
- Check in as a person, not just a task-router: ask about their life, note personal details (kids, interests) if shared, do a periodic (monthly/quarterly) personal check-in call.
- Praise wins specifically and share credit publicly when a video/channel does unusually well; bonuses for outlier performance if finances allow, but even verbal recognition matters.
- Project confidence and long-term stability about the channel/project's future when talking to your team, this affects their sense of job security and effort. But:
- Do NOT get overly friendly/blur the boss-employee line. Stay warm but maintain "you're still the client" framing, this matters specifically when things go bad (firing, disputes, raises).

## Security and risk management (directly relevant to Ace Knows a Guy team build-out)
- Never give full personal-account credentials, use team/shared accounts with limited-balance cards for tools like Epidemic Sounds, etc.
- Assume editors/team members CAN cause real damage even without channel-owner access: privating videos, changing thumbnails/titles to something that triggers a takedown, holding channel access "hostage" on a bad exit.
- The moment a team relationship turns problematic (before firing, at first sign of trouble), immediately strip access to software, folders, drives, Trello, etc. Don't wait for the actual firing conversation.
- Even a departure that isn't malicious is a leak risk: a disgruntled or just-departed team member can take your workflow/style to a competitor. Don't share revenue figures with team members unless functionally necessary (avoids premature raise requests AND avoids morale damage if revenue dips).
- Use lightweight contracts/NDAs even though they're weakly enforceable cross-border, the existence of a signed contract has a real deterrent effect.
- Get invoices BEFORE paying, every time, from day one. This prevents payment disputes and is often required for tax/expense documentation. Critically: get the habit established immediately, because a departing/fired team member may ghost you on invoice requests needed for tax records if you wait until the split to ask.
